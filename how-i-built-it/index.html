How I built it with AI

Over six weeks I delivered a climate engagement for a fictional mid-market private equity fund, working with Claude Code throughout. The deliverables are on the week pages. This page is about the other thing I built along the way: a way of working that makes AI-produced analysis checkable, and how it grew from no review at all to gated, independently checked delivery.

The short version

AI made it fast to produce analysis. It did not make the analysis right. Early on, the same session that wrote a number also checked it, and that is not a check. Most of what I learned was about separating those two jobs: writing down the rules before seeing the results, giving reviewers their own narrow briefs and no power to edit, making every check prove it can fail, and keeping a record that a sceptical reader could follow from a headline figure back to its source.

By the last weeks the work ran on a small framework: a manifest of gates, a hash-chained audit log of every script run, registers of every decision and finding, review packets sized to each reviewer, and freezes that pin the signed files. The point of all of it is the same as the point of the climate work: numbers a buyer's diligence team can trust.

How the practice grew, week by week

  1. Week 1: inquiry and pitch

    No review step

    Week 1 had no recorded review. When I reviewed it for this portfolio, two independent reviewers found the pitch offered the other six companies at "a fraction of the cost" or close to marginal cost on three slides, while another slide quoted them at the same rate. A small error, and the reason everything after it changed.

  2. Week 2: data audit

    The first review rewrote the process

    Six read-only reviews, by four kinds of reviewer agent, went over the audit memo, the data requests and the deck. They returned 44 items: 17 must-fix, 10 decisions for me, 9 should-fix and 8 changes to the method. The process reviewer's core point: everything had been built and checked by the same pass. I revised the audit method around it: thresholds registered before any data is reviewed, a produced inventory of every file with its data vintage, and a final stage split into running the checks and reviewing the work.

  3. Week 3: carbon inventory, 138 sites

    Rules locked before the numbers

    Fourteen calculation rules were pre-registered and locked before the inventory was built; every later amendment was logged. Script runs went into a hash-chained audit log. An independent recalculation rebuilt 376 base and 153 sensitivity lines from the raw template. Four gates were signed and frozen with file hashes.

    The last gate, G6, which covers the client deck and the ESG scorecard, was not run because Week 4 began; the Week 3 deck says so.

  4. Week 4: materiality and initiatives

    From a process to a framework

    I turned the Week 3 process into a reusable kit: a manifest of gates and review lanes, a check registry, per-reviewer packets and freezes. Every check had to name a deliberately wrong input it must reject. All eight gates, G0 to G7, were signed. The first gate took four reviewer passes and about ten document versions, which led to the next change.

  5. Week 5: physical risk

    Checks that do not share the builder's blind spots

    Kernel v2 added a pre-review lint and required checks written independently of the code they test. The physical-loss model was replayed against nine events the companies had reported, with severity fixed in advance: five passed on the first run. Two assumptions were then narrowed and two events left untested, after which all seven tested events pass. The method note reports the first run, because the final seven is a tuned fit, not a validation.

  6. Week 6: LP disclosure

    Delivery as a repeatable service

    The LP report ran a 22-item QA (18 pass, 4 not applicable, none failing), checks re-ran the whole build from pinned inputs in a fresh folder, and all four of its gates were signed.

  7. After the cohort: this portfolio

    Reviewing my own work for a public reader

    Each week was reviewed again for public use, with assurance and presentation reviewers, method review for the calculation weeks, and a browser check on a phone-width screen. For the last week I delegated the whole review to Claude as orchestrator: it chose a model for each task (the stronger one for method review, a faster one for tracing and building) and checked each reviewer claim against its source before acting on it, and I signed off the result.

  • v1 to v2: 13 KB to 4 KB without losing a rule. Folder layout and week-by-week tooling moved to reference files.
  • v3: a dating convention, because the case data room runs on its own fictional clock while the cohort runs on the real one.
  • v4: an order of authority for when documents conflict. Adding it changed a file that a previous gate had frozen, so the next gate's check failed and needed a logged override: the rules file is itself evidence.
  • v5 and v6: a context-economy section. The live build record had reached about 110,000 tokens, so "do not read it whole" became a rule and lookup-by-id tools were built the same day. The full v4 wording was kept in a reference file, with a script that proves nothing was lost.
  • v7 and v8: sessions start from the active week's generated state file (about 700 tokens), and files may be edited in place with a logged change until they enter a review or a freeze.

Three rules never changed: every claim must move EBITDA, reduce deal risk or support exit readiness; every deliverable ships with its method and data so the client can re-run it without us; and "run the council" with no context means ask which council.

Two councils, and the reviewers

I used two kinds of review and kept them apart. For open decisions, a council of five advisors argues the question and a chair gives a verdict. For deliverables, six project agents each check one thing, with read-only access so they can report but never quietly fix.

ReviewerChecks
Assurance auditEvery number, claim and citation against its source
Method reviewWhether the calculation is right and conforms to the GHG Protocol
Process reviewHow the work was scoped, sequenced, verified and packaged
Deck qualityWhether the deliverable answers the brief in the client's language
Client voiceHow it lands with the named client reader
Sweep adjudicatorA blind, quote-backed reading of ambiguous data-room passages

From Week 3 on, every change to an agent's instructions was shown to me and approved before it was saved.

The AI's premise was wrong

The build assumed the site coordinates were city-level only. I pointed to the course material saying we held coordinates for all 138 facilities; the decision was reversed and 13 location conflicts became client findings.

The report printed an intensity of 13.7, but the emissions and revenue printed beside it gave 12.7. The 13.7 used full-year emissions that were not shown. The audience reviewer did the division, and the report now prints the numerator and explains the difference.

What I would keep

  1. Separate building from checking. A different reviewer, a narrow brief, no power to edit.
  2. Every check must be able to fail. Seed a wrong input and confirm the check catches it; otherwise a pass means nothing.
  3. Write the rules before seeing the results. Pre-registered thresholds stop the analysis bending toward a convenient answer.
  4. Record decisions in the owner's words, when they are made. The conversation does not survive; the log does.
  5. Treat context as a budget. Small state files, lookup by id, packets sized to each reviewer.
  6. Keep judgment human. The AI drafted, built and checked at speed; the calls that mattered, including overruling it, were mine.

Honest limits

Week 1 had no review during the cohort. Week 3's last gate, covering the client deck and scorecard, was never run. Week 5's planned red-team review was deferred. Twenty-one findings, 10 in Week 3 and 11 in Week 4, were left not closed when each week ended. In Week 6, the framework indices claimed 16 of 48 disclosure points as met; on review for this portfolio, 9 of 48 hold, and the pages now show both.
